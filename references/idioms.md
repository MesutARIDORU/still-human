# Saying it with an idiom

An idiom can do in four words what a model needs a sentence for: "we're back to square one" instead of "we have returned to the initial stage of the process". Used well, idioms are one of the clearest signs of a person writing. Used badly they're the opposite: models reach for the same handful of tired figures ("at the end of the day", "a double-edged sword", "the tip of the iceberg") and mix them ("we need to nip this in the bud before it snowballs out of the woodwork").

This file gives:
- how much idiom each text type can take,
- what to check before you use one,
- idioms grouped by the work situation they fit, each with the stiff phrasing it replaces and an example,
- the dead clichés models overuse,
- idioms people commonly mangle, and pairs they confuse.

Marks: **(UK)** or **(US)** where an idiom is mainly used on one side of the Atlantic; no mark means both. Follow the text's spelling system (`style.md`).

## Which text, how much

| Text type | Dose |
|---|---|
| Poetry, legal writing, technical specification | None |
| Technical documentation, academic writing, news writing | Almost none. A plain, established phrasal idiom ("rule of thumb", "trade-off") at most |
| Business correspondence to customers | Rarely, and only plain ones ("on track", "in the loop" inside a company) |
| Internal business correspondence | One per message, if it's the team's way of talking |
| Essay and opinion, personal writing, social media | One per paragraph at most |
| Literary narrative | In characters' speech, to characterise them; sparingly in narration |
| Marketing copy | A fresh twist can work; a stock one makes copy sound generic |

## Before you use one

- **Is it the reader's idiom?** A British idiom in a US text (or the reverse) sounds borrowed. Some don't travel at all for international readers.
- **Does it fit the register?** "Throw someone under the bus" is fine in a column, wrong in an HR letter.
- **One per paragraph.** Two idioms side by side compete, and mixed metaphors start there.
- **Is it alive?** If the phrase is on the dead-cliché list below, say it plainly instead.
- **Is it correct?** Check the mangled list. "Free reign", "tow the line" and "for all intensive purposes" stand out.
- **Does it add anything?** If the plain version is just as short, use the plain version.

## By work situation

Each line: **idiom** — the stiff phrasing it replaces — an example.

### Effort and difficulty
- **uphill battle** — "a significant challenge requiring sustained effort" — "Getting budget approval this year is an uphill battle."
- **easier said than done** — "while conceptually straightforward, implementation may prove complex" — "Cutting meetings in half is easier said than done."
- **heavy lifting** — "the most resource-intensive aspects of the work" — "The data team did the heavy lifting on the migration."
- **learning curve** — "an initial period of adaptation" — "There's a learning curve, but most people are comfortable within a week [if the text says so]."
- **bite off more than you can chew** — "overcommit relative to available capacity" — "We bit off more than we could chew with three launches in one quarter."
- **back to the drawing board** — "a return to the initial design phase" — "The prototype failed the safety test, so it's back to the drawing board."

### Speed and timing
- **hit the ground running** — "commence work at full productivity immediately" — "The new hires hit the ground running." (often a job-ad cliché; avoid there)
- **in the nick of time** — "immediately prior to the deadline" — "The fix went out in the nick of time."
- **at short notice** (UK) / **on short notice** (US) — "with minimal advance warning" — "The venue changed at short notice."
- **in the pipeline** — "currently under development" — "Two more features are in the pipeline."
- **drag your feet** — "proceed at a suboptimal pace" — "The supplier has been dragging its feet on the contract."
- **a race against time** — "a time-critical endeavour" — use only when it really is.

### Delay and stalling
- **on the back burner** — "temporarily deprioritised" — "The redesign is on the back burner until the spring."
- **kick the can down the road** — "defer the decision to a future date" — "Another review just kicks the can down the road."
- **on hold** — "in a paused state" — "Hiring is on hold."
- **in limbo** — "in a state of uncertainty pending resolution" — "The project has been in limbo since the merger."

### Results: what worked and what didn't
- **pay off** — "yield positive returns" — "The extra testing paid off."
- **fall flat** — "fail to achieve the intended impact" — "The campaign fell flat with younger readers."
- **back to square one** — "returned to the initial stage" — "The vote failed, so we're back to square one."
- **hit or miss** — "of inconsistent quality" — "The old search was hit or miss."
- **a long shot** — "an outcome with a low probability of success" — "Winning the tender is a long shot."
- **come up short** — "fail to meet the target" — "Sales came up short in March [by how much?]."

### Importance and exaggeration
- **the elephant in the room** — "a significant issue that has not been addressed" — "The elephant in the room is the budget." (tired; use sparingly)
- **make or break** — "of decisive importance" — "The first month is make or break for a new café."
- **a drop in the ocean** (UK) / **a drop in the bucket** (US) — "a negligible contribution relative to the overall need" — "The grant is a drop in the ocean."
- **blow out of proportion** — "overstate the significance" — "The delay was blown out of proportion."

### Mistakes, risk and caution
- **play it safe** — "adopt a risk-averse approach" — "We'll play it safe and release on Monday."
- **cut corners** — "compromise on quality to save time or cost" — "We didn't cut corners on the security review."
- **a close call** — "a near-miss incident" — "The outage was a close call."
- **teething problems** (UK) / **growing pains** — "initial operational difficulties" — "The new system had teething problems in its first week."
- **cross that bridge when we come to it** — "address that contingency if and when it arises".

### Money and cost
- **cost an arm and a leg** — "be prohibitively expensive" — informal only.
- **tighten our belts** — "implement cost-reduction measures" — "We'll have to tighten our belts next quarter."
- **foot the bill** — "bear the financial cost" — "Who foots the bill for the repairs?"
- **break even** — "reach the point of zero profit and loss" — "The shop broke even in its second year [if the text says so]."
- **ballpark figure** (mainly US) — "an approximate estimate" — "Can you give me a ballpark figure?"

### Teams, customers and trust
- **on the same page** — "in alignment regarding objectives" — "Let's make sure everyone's on the same page before Friday."
- **in the loop** — "kept informed of developments" — "Please keep finance in the loop."
- **pull your weight** — "contribute an appropriate share of effort".
- **go the extra mile** — "exceed expectations in service delivery" — tired in marketing; fine in a thank-you note with a specific example.
- **earn someone's trust** — "establish credibility" — plain and alive.

### Talk, meetings and persuasion
- **get to the point** — "address the core matter directly".
- **food for thought** — "content warranting further consideration".
- **beat around the bush** — "communicate indirectly" — "I won't beat around the bush: the project is cancelled."
- **sound someone out** — "ascertain someone's views informally" — "I'll sound out the team before we decide."
- **talk shop** — "discuss work-related matters outside work".

### Uncertainty, decisions and direction
- **up in the air** — "not yet determined" — "The date is still up in the air."
- **on the fence** — "undecided" — "Half the committee is on the fence."
- **a judgement call** (UK spelling) / **a judgment call** (US) — "a decision requiring discretion" — "Whether to delay is a judgement call."
- **a step in the right direction** — "incremental progress towards the objective" — tired; say what the step was.
- **rule of thumb** — "a general heuristic" — "As a rule of thumb, keep sentences under 25 words."

### Feeling: for narrative and personal writing
- **have butterflies in your stomach** — "experience nervous anticipation".
- **at a loss for words** — "unable to articulate a response".
- **a lump in your throat** — "a feeling of being moved".
- **on cloud nine** — "extremely happy" — informal.
- **down in the dumps** — "feeling low" — informal.

In fiction, prefer a concrete detail to any of these (F33); in a character's speech, an idiom can show who they are.

## Dead clichés

Models lean on these because they are the most frequent figures in their training text. In most texts, say the plain thing instead.

| Cliché | Say instead |
|---|---|
| at the end of the day | finally, in the end, or nothing |
| a double-edged sword | it has costs and benefits: name them |
| the tip of the iceberg | there's more: say what |
| a game-changer | what it changes, for whom |
| think outside the box | try something different: say what |
| a perfect storm | several problems at once: name them |
| move the needle | change [the figure] |
| low-hanging fruit | the easiest fixes |
| a level playing field | equal rules for [whom] |
| navigate uncharted waters | do something new: what |
| it's not rocket science | it's simple |
| the new normal | how things are now |
| when all is said and done | in the end |
| a rollercoaster of emotions | name the feelings, or show them |
| only time will tell | we don't know yet |
| hit the ground running | start straight away |
| a win-win situation | both sides gain: say what |
| push the envelope | go further than before: how |
| at this point in time | now |
| in today's fast-paced world | (delete: T6) |

## Mangled idioms and eggcorns

Common slips. Getting these wrong is human, but a rewrite shouldn't introduce them, and an editor should fix them politely.

| Wrong | Right |
|---|---|
| free reign | free rein |
| tow the line | toe the line |
| for all intensive purposes | for all intents and purposes |
| hone in on | home in on (although "hone in" is now common in US usage) |
| I could care less | I couldn't care less |
| case and point | case in point |
| nip it in the butt | nip it in the bud |
| one in the same | one and the same |
| a mute point | a moot point |
| pour over the report | pore over the report |
| peak your interest | pique your interest |
| baited breath | bated breath |
| sneak peak | sneak peek |
| deep-seeded | deep-seated |
| wreck havoc | wreak havoc |
| should of | should have |

## Pairs people confuse

| Pair | Difference |
|---|---|
| affect / effect | affect is usually the verb, effect the noun ("it affects sales"; "the effect on sales") |
| comprise / compose | the whole comprises the parts; the parts compose the whole ("comprised of" is common but criticised) |
| fewer / less | fewer for things you count, less for amounts ("fewer staff", "less time") |
| flesh out / flush out | flesh out a plan (add detail); flush out a problem (force it into view) |
| principal / principle | the principal reason; a matter of principle |
| complement / compliment | colours complement each other; you compliment someone |
| discreet / discrete | discreet means tactful; discrete means separate |
| ensure / insure | ensure that something happens; insure against a loss |
| imply / infer | the speaker implies; the listener infers |
| stationary / stationery | not moving / paper and pens |
| practice / practise (UK) | in UK English practice is the noun, practise the verb; US uses practice for both |
| licence / license (UK) | in UK English licence is the noun, license the verb; US uses license for both |

## Mixed metaphors

Two images in one sentence fight each other: "We need to grab the bull by the horns before the ship sails." "That's a slippery slope to a can of worms." If you use an idiom, keep the rest of the sentence literal.
