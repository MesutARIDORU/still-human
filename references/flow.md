# How English flows

Removing a pattern leaves a gap. This file is about what goes into it: the tools English prose uses to move, link and land. Each rule has a short explanation, the text types it suits, and before/after examples from different fields. As everywhere in this skill, the after text adds no information that isn't in the before text.

## Building sentences

### F1 · Put the news first
Readers decide in the first line whether to keep reading. Put the main point there: the decision, the change, the date, the answer. Background comes after. In business writing this is sometimes called "bottom line up front"; in journalism, the lede.

**Before:**
> As part of our ongoing review of working practices, and following feedback from many of you in the staff survey, we have been looking at how we use the office. After careful consideration, we have decided to close the third floor from 1 March.

**After:**
> We're closing the third floor from 1 March. The decision follows our review of how we use the office and your feedback in the staff survey.

- *Public sector:* "Following a review of eligibility criteria, the council has updated its guidance, and as a result, you may now be able to apply." → "You may now be able to apply: the council has changed who is eligible."

**Where not:** in fiction, essays and some personal writing, delaying the point is a legitimate choice.

### F2 · Name the actor, then the action
English is clearest when the subject is the one who acts and the verb is what they do. "The team fixed the bug" beats "The bug was fixed". Use the passive when the actor is unknown, unimportant, or when the thing acted on is the topic (a methods section, a history). If the text doesn't say who acted, don't invent someone.

**Before:**
> Your request has been received and will be reviewed by a member of the support team.

**After:**
> We've got your request, and someone from the support team will review it.

- *Software:* "Priorities are set automatically." → "The dashboard sets priorities automatically." (only because the text says the dashboard does it)

### F3 · Use the verb, not the noun made from it
Many abstract nouns are verbs in disguise: decision, analysis, implementation, consideration, assessment, completion. Turn them back into verbs and the weak verbs around them (make, conduct, carry out, give) fall away.

**Before:**
> The board will give consideration to the proposal and make a determination following the completion of the review.

**After:**
> The board will consider the proposal and decide once the review is finished.

- *Health:* "Undertake an assessment of the patient's mobility" → "Assess the patient's mobility."

### F4 · Keep the subject close to its verb
A long phrase between the subject and its verb makes the reader hold the subject in mind. Move the phrase to its own sentence or to the end.

**Before:**
> The new policy, which was drafted after six months of consultation with staff, unions and external advisers and approved by the board in June, takes effect next week.

**After:**
> The new policy takes effect next week. It was drafted after six months of consultation with staff, unions and external advisers, and the board approved it in June.

### F5 · Save the end of the sentence for what matters
In English the end of a sentence is the stress position: the reader's attention lands there. Put the new or important element last, and the familiar part earlier.

**Before:**
> A 2.85% rate will apply to your account from 1 December.

**After:**
> From 1 December, your account will earn 2.85%.

## Linking sentences

### F6 · Start from what the reader already knows
Begin a sentence with something the previous sentence mentioned, then add the new information. Prose built this way reads as a chain, not as a list of separate statements.

**Before:**
> We tested two layouts. Version B was chosen by 61% of users in a two-week trial.

**After:**
> We tested two layouts. In a two-week trial, 61% of users chose version B.

### F7 · Let the idea make the transition
"Moreover", "Furthermore", "Additionally" announce a link without making it. Usually the second sentence already follows from the first; if it doesn't, write the link in words ("Because of that…", "The same thing happened in…").

**Before:**
> The trains will run every 15 minutes. Moreover, tickets will cost less. Furthermore, the station will open earlier.

**After:**
> Trains will run every 15 minutes, tickets will cost less, and the station will open earlier.

### F8 · "And", "but" and "so" can start a sentence
Starting a sentence with a coordinating conjunction is long-established English usage. It is often lighter than "However" or "Therefore" and keeps the rhythm of speech.

**Before:**
> The trial went well. However, we are not ready to launch. Therefore, we will run a second phase.

**After:**
> The trial went well. But we aren't ready to launch, so we'll run a second phase.

**Where not:** specifications, legal text, formal academic prose.

### F9 · Introduce once, then refer
Give the full name once, then a short name, then a pronoun, and keep the same short name throughout (S4). Don't rotate synonyms.

**Before:**
> The Regional Transport Authority announced the plan on Monday. The Regional Transport Authority said the plan would cost £4m. The organisation expects the body's board to approve it.

**After:**
> The Regional Transport Authority announced the plan on Monday. The authority said it would cost £4m and expects its board to approve it.

### F10 · Join actions that belong together
A run of short sentences describing one sequence of actions can usually become one sentence with "and", commas or a time clause (S2).

**Before:**
> She opened the e-mail. She read the first line. She closed the laptop.

**After:**
> She opened the e-mail, read the first line and closed the laptop.

## Rhythm

### F11 · Put a short sentence next to a long one
The ear notices variety. A short sentence after a long one lands harder; a long one after several short ones lets the reader breathe. This is the most direct fix for S1.

**Before:**
> The survey had 1,200 responses this year. Most respondents were happy with the new hours. A smaller group asked for later opening on Thursdays. We will trial that from September.

**After:**
> The survey had 1,200 responses this year, and most people were happy with the new hours, though a smaller group asked for later opening on Thursdays. We'll trial that from September.

### F12 · Let sentence length follow the tension
Long sentences for build-up, context and reasoning; short ones for the turn, the decision, the shock. In narrative this is the main tool for pace.

**Before:**
> The test results arrived on Thursday, and they showed that the system had failed under load, which meant the launch would have to be postponed.

**After:**
> The test results arrived on Thursday. The system had failed under load. The launch would have to wait.

### F13 · Vary the shape, not just the length
Rhythm also comes from different sentence shapes: a question (sparingly), a sentence that opens with a time or place, a short fragment that does work, a list sentence, a sentence with the subject late. Varying only the length while every sentence stays subject–verb–object still sounds mechanical.

**Before:**
> The café opens at seven. The café serves breakfast until eleven. The café closes at four.

**After:**
> The café opens at seven and serves breakfast until eleven. By four it's shut.

## Emphasis

### F14 · Let structure carry the emphasis
Emphasis comes from position (the end of the sentence, the start of the paragraph), from a short sentence among long ones, and from deliberate repetition. Not from "Here's the thing:", bold, or "truly".

**Before:**
> Here's the thing: the deadline is truly non-negotiable.

**After:**
> The deadline won't move.

### F15 · Say it first, name it afterwards
If something needs a label ("this is the risk", "that's the trade-off"), give the content first and the label after. Announcing first (R2) builds a drumroll.

**Before:**
> The biggest risk? Our supplier only has one factory.

**After:**
> Our supplier only has one factory. That's the biggest risk.

### F16 · Don't ask what you're about to answer
A question the writer answers in the next sentence (R3) stretches one statement into two. Either state the point, or ask a question you leave open for the reader.

**Before:**
> So what does this mean for you? It means your bill will go down.

**After:**
> Your bill will go down.

## Certainty

### F17 · Match certainty to the evidence
"Will", "should", "may", "might", "could" and "is associated with" carry different levels of certainty. Keep the source's level. Never turn "may help" into "helps" or "will" into "may". In health, finance and law, this is a matter of safety and liability, not style.

**Before:**
> The supplement may support joint health.

**Wrong rewrite:**
> The supplement supports joint health.

**Right rewrite:**
> The supplement may help your joints.

### F18 · One hedge per claim
A claim that needs hedging needs one hedge. "May potentially", "could possibly help to", "might arguably" hedge the hedge (W6).

**Before:**
> These changes could potentially possibly reduce waiting times.

**After:**
> These changes could reduce waiting times.

### F19 · Pick the reporting verb that is true
"Said" is neutral and nearly invisible; that is its strength. "Claimed" casts doubt, "admitted" implies a confession, "revealed" implies a secret, "noted" and "highlighted" imply the thing is true. Choose the verb that matches what the source shows; when in doubt, "said".

**Before:**
> The company revealed that profits rose 4%.

**After:**
> The company said profits rose 4%.

### F20 · Step back with the past perfect, then return
In past-tense narration, the past perfect ("had gone") marks an earlier time. Use it to step into a flashback, then switch back to the simple past; a whole flashback in "had" is heavy.

**Before:**
> She had been to the house once before. She had driven up on a Sunday. She had parked by the gate. She had waited an hour.

**After:**
> She had been to the house once before. She drove up on a Sunday, parked by the gate and waited an hour.

## Word texture

### F21 · Short plain verbs
English has two vocabularies for most actions: a short, older one (buy, help, start, end, need, use, get, show) and a longer Latinate one (purchase, assist, commence, terminate, require, utilise, obtain, demonstrate). Most texts read better with the first. Government style guides in the UK and US say the same (`style.md`).

**Before:**
> Customers who wish to purchase additional storage are required to obtain approval prior to commencing the upgrade.

**After:**
> If you want to buy more storage, you need approval before you start the upgrade.

**Where not:** a legal defined term, or a field where the long word is the precise one ("terminate a contract", "administer a drug").

### F22 · Phrasal verbs where the register allows
Phrasal verbs (set up, find out, roll out, sign up, look into, carry on) are the natural spoken form of many actions. They make instructions, e-mails and blog posts sound like a person. Formal and international texts may prefer the single word, since phrasal verbs are harder for non-native readers; decide by audience.

**Before:**
> To establish your account, register and then investigate the settings.

**After:**
> To set up your account, sign up, then look through the settings.

### F23 · Contractions where the register allows
"It's", "we'll", "don't", "you're" are normal in e-mails, blogs, documentation, social posts and most business writing; their total absence in a friendly text sounds stiff. Leave them out of legal text, specifications, most academic writing and formal letters. Some style guides avoid negative contractions ("can't") for clarity; others prefer them because a scanning reader can miss "not" (`style.md`). Follow the house style.

**Before (team e-mail):**
> We are moving the meeting to Thursday. It is the only day that does not clash with the release.

**After:**
> We're moving the meeting to Thursday. It's the only day that doesn't clash with the release.

### F24 · Name the concrete thing
Abstract nouns (solutions, offerings, resources, functionality, experience) hide what the text is about. Name the thing: the app, the form, the refund, the bus. If the text doesn't say what the thing is, ask.

**Before:**
> We offer a range of resources to support your wellbeing journey.

**After:**
> We offer [what, exactly: counselling sessions, an app, a helpline?].

### F25 · Cut what changes nothing
If a word or phrase can go without changing the meaning, it should go: "very", "really", "actually", "in order", "the fact that", "in terms of", "at this point in time", "currently" next to a present tense.

**Before:**
> We are currently in the process of actually reviewing the applications at this point in time.

**After:**
> We're reviewing the applications.

## The reader

### F26 · Fix the address
Decide how the text talks to the reader ("you" or "customers") and how it talks about the writer ("we" or the organisation's name), and keep it. Swapping between them sentence to sentence is a sign of assembled text.

**Before:**
> Customers can update their details online. You will need your reference number. Harbour Bank will confirm the change by e-mail, and we aim to do this within two days.

**After:**
> You can update your details online with your reference number. We'll confirm the change by e-mail, within two days where we can.

**Note:** "within two days where we can" keeps "aim to" from the source; it doesn't promise more.

### F27 · Talk to the reader where the genre lets you
Instructions, blog posts, essays and many public-sector texts are clearer in the second person and the imperative: "Bring photo ID", not "Applicants are required to present identification". News, academic and legal texts usually don't address the reader.

**Before:**
> Applicants should ensure that all required documents are provided at the time of application.

**After:**
> Send all the documents with your application.

### F28 · Stance words, sparingly
Words like "frankly", "admittedly", "still", "at least", "of course", "oddly" show the writer's attitude and give prose a voice. One or two in an essay is personality; one per sentence is a tic (and a tell of over-correction, E6).

**Before:**
> The new version is slower. It is, however, more stable.

**After:**
> The new version is slower. It's more stable, at least.

### F29 · Count honestly
Don't round lists to three (S7) or reach for "a myriad of". If there are two reasons, give two. Approximate where the source is approximate: "two or three people", "a handful of", "most of the team".

**Before:**
> The change will improve speed, reliability and user satisfaction.

**After (if the text supports only speed):**
> The change will make the app faster.

## Instructions and business text

### F30 · Put an example next to a rule
A rule with an example is understood the first time. Keep the example from the text or ask for one; don't invent figures.

**Before:**
> File names must follow the naming convention.

**After:**
> File names must follow the naming convention, for example [an example file name?].

### F31 · Short instruction, one reason
One action per step, in the imperative, with at most one reason. If a reason is needed, it goes after the instruction.

**Before:**
> In order to ensure the security of your account and protect your personal data, it is recommended that you consider enabling two-factor authentication.

**After:**
> We recommend turning on two-factor authentication to protect your account.

**Note:** the source recommends; it doesn't require, so the rewrite keeps the recommendation (F17). If the writer confirms it's required, the imperative is clearer still: "Turn on two-factor authentication."

### F32 · Lead with what the reader came for
Every practical text answers a few questions: what is it, when, where, how much, what do I do. Put those first and make them easy to find. Context and courtesy come after.

**Before:**
> We're delighted to invite you to our annual community event, which has become a much-loved tradition. This year it will take place on Saturday 14 June, from 11am to 4pm, at Riverside Park.

**After:**
> Saturday 14 June, 11am to 4pm, Riverside Park: you're invited to our annual community event.

## Narrative and dialogue

### F33 · Show the feeling; don't name it
"She felt sad" tells; a gesture, a choice, a detail she notices shows. Use what's already in the scene first. If you add a physical detail, list it in the change table as "added detail" (see SKILL.md, principle 1).

**Before:**
> He was nervous about the interview.

**After:**
> He checked the address twice on the bus, though he'd written it on his hand. *(added detail)*

### F34 · Let a character's thought slip into the narration
Free indirect style lets the narration take on a character's words without "she thought": "Another meeting. Of course." It brings the reader inside the character and cuts tags.

**Before:**
> She thought to herself that it was typical of him to be late again.

**After:**
> Late again. Typical.

### F35 · Keep dialogue tags plain
"Said" and "asked" disappear on the page; "she breathed", "he husked", "she murmured softly" draw attention to themselves. Often no tag is needed: an action beat ("Tom put his cup down.") shows who is speaking. Don't add adverbs to tags.

**Before:**
> "She left you the house," Tom murmured softly, his eyes searching hers.

**After:**
> "She left you the house," Tom said.
